Clinical communication for Deaf patients — demonstration summary
October 2026

In most Deaf patient encounters, no interpreter is present. The alternative in use today is a family member, a notepad, or a nurse improvising. Unmute is being built to be what is actually there.

What the published evidence shows

63.9%of emergency physicians used a family member to interpret
16.9%had the patient write on paper
8.4%used any professional interpretation

Akeely et al., Communication Challenges While Dealing With a Deaf Patient in the Emergency Department, Cureus, 2022. n = 166 emergency physicians. In the same study, 74.1% of departments reported no policy for Deaf patients and 90.4% of staff knew no sign language.

How it works today

  1. The clinician asks a question from a fixed clinical set — pain level, understanding, visit history, interpreter need.
  2. The patient signs the answer.
  3. The app proposes its three best interpretations.
  4. The patient confirms which one is correct.
  5. The clinician sees a structured, patient-confirmed record.

The model never decides. It proposes, the patient chooses. That is a product principle, not a fallback: no automated system should speak for a Deaf patient.

What it is

  • A communication layer for routine encounters
  • Runs in a browser on hardware already in the room
  • No installation, no integration, no new device
  • Designed so no video or patient data leaves the device

What it is not

  • Not a replacement for a qualified interpreter
  • Not for consent, diagnosis, mental health or end-of-life conversations
  • Not a medical device, and not clinically validated
  • Not making any accuracy claim at this stage

What a first trial would involve

  • One department, roughly ten practitioners, four weeks
  • No EHR integration and no procurement — it runs on a tablet or laptop already on the ward
  • We observe where it helps and, more importantly, where it fails
  • You keep every finding; we publish the failure cases

What we are asking for today

Thirty minutes with the person responsible for accessibility, patient experience, or emergency nursing — and a candid view of what happens now when a Deaf patient arrives. We are not asking for a purchase.
