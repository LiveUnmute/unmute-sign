# Hospital Visit Playbook

**Purpose:** turn a laptop demo into a design partner, without overclaiming.
**Time:** read this twice before the first visit.

---

## Before you go

**Pack**
- Laptop, fully charged, plus the charger
- Phone hotspot as backup — the demo needs the internet once to load the hand model, then caches
- The printed one-pager (`onepager.html`, print to A4) — three copies, not thirty
- A notebook. **Do not take notes on the laptop during the meeting.** It reads as data collection
- This playbook, printed

**Test the night before**
- Open the demo, allow the camera, and sign 1–5 until the candidates are stable
- Check the lighting in the room you'll use. Backlight kills hand tracking
- Run it once with the wifi off to see exactly what still works

**Know what fails.** The recogniser is a geometric prototype, not a trained model. It reads five handshapes and three gestures. If it misfires in front of a clinician, that is a normal outcome, not a catastrophe — say so and move on. The demo also runs in tap mode with no camera at all.

---

## Who to ask for

Not the CEO. Not the CIO on the first visit.

| Priority | Role | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Emergency department charge nurse / nurse manager** | Sees the problem weekly. Has no budget, but is the best source of truth |
| 2 | **Patient experience / patient access lead** | Owns complaints and satisfaction scores, which is where this problem shows up |
| 3 | **ADA / accessibility coordinator** | Often exists in larger US systems; has a mandate and sometimes a budget |
| 4 | **Deaf staff member, or the interpreter agency they contract** | The most important conversation you can have, and the one most founders skip |

**Ask this on arrival:** *"Is there anyone here who is Deaf, or who works with Deaf patients regularly, who I should be talking to?"* If the answer is yes, that person matters more than the person who agreed to see you.

## The five-minute demo script

Do not open with slides. Open with the laptop closed and one question.

### Minute 0 — the question

> "Can I ask something first? When a Deaf patient comes through your department — what actually happens? Walk me through it."

**Then be quiet.** Let them talk. You will learn more in these three minutes than in the rest of the visit. Write down their exact words; they are worth more than any statistic in your deck.

### Minute 1 — name the gap

> "The reason I'm asking: when researchers surveyed emergency physicians, under ten per cent used any professional interpretation. Sixty-four per cent used a family member. I'm not here to tell you your team is unusual — I think that's the norm everywhere, because interpreters aren't available on demand."

### Minute 2 — open the laptop

> "Let me show you what we've built. It's a prototype, and I'll show you its limits as we go."

Run the intake. Sign the answers yourself. Let the **clinician panel fill in** — that's the moment that lands.

### Minute 3 — the principle

> "Notice the patient confirms every answer. The AI never decides. It gives its best three, and the patient picks. We built it that way because no automated system should speak for a Deaf person."

### Minute 4 — the limits, said out loud

> "What this is not: it doesn't replace an interpreter, and it can't. It's for pain levels, understanding, basic history — not consent, not a diagnosis, not a difficult conversation. And it's not clinically validated. I'm not going to give you an accuracy number, because I don't have one yet."

**Saying this yourself is the whole play.** Every clinician in the room is waiting to see whether you'll oversell. The founder who names the limits first is the one who gets a second meeting.

### Minute 5 — the ask

> "I'm not selling anything today. What I'd like is a conversation about whether a four-week trial in one department would be useful — and honestly, whether you think it would help or get in the way."

## The seven questions to ask them

Ask these. They matter more than the demo.

1. When a Deaf patient arrives, who decides how communication happens?
2. Has an interpreter ever been requested and not arrived? What happened?
3. What do staff actually do when no interpreter is available?
4. Does anyone review those encounters afterwards?
5. Who, if anyone, owns this as a problem?
6. Has this ever appeared in a complaint or a safety report?
7. If a tool like this existed, whose budget would it come from — and would it have to go through IT?

Question 7 is the one that tells you whether your business model is real.

---

## What you must not say

| Never say | Because |
|---|---|
| "We replace interpreters" / "you won't need interpreters" | Untrue, legally reckless for them, and it turns the Deaf community against you |
| "HIPAA compliant" | You are not certified, and there is no BAA. Say instead: *"the design means there's no patient data leaving the device"* |
| "FDA approved" or anything implying it | It is not a medical device and has not been reviewed |
| "Our accuracy is X%" | You have no accuracy figure. Say so |
| "We're working with [hospital]" | Unless they have signed something. They have not |
| Anything about a patient you have seen | Never discuss identifiable patients in any room |

**One more:** if they ask you to try it on a real patient today — decline politely. Say you're not ready for that, and mean it. A prototype failing on a patient in front of a nurse manager ends the relationship permanently.

## The regulatory question, if it comes up

Someone senior may ask whether this is regulated software. The honest answer:

> "It relays a patient's own answers back to the clinician — it doesn't diagnose, recommend treatment, or interpret clinical findings. My understanding is that keeps it outside medical-device regulation, but I haven't had that confirmed by counsel yet, and I won't deploy in a clinical setting until I have."

Then move on. If you don't know, say you don't know. In a hospital, a confident wrong answer about regulation is disqualifying.

## After the visit — within two hours

Write these down while it's fresh:

- **Their exact words** describing what happens now
- The one moment in the demo where their expression changed
- Every objection, verbatim
- Whether they asked "how much" (a buying signal) or "is this safe" (a blocker)
- Whether they named a person who could champion it
- Whether they offered anything — a follow-up, an intro, a walk around the department

**Then send one short email within 24 hours.** Three paragraphs: what you heard, what you'll change because of it, and one specific question. No deck attached. No pitch.

---

## What counts as success

Not a sale. Not a pilot agreement.

| Outcome | Read |
|---|---|
| They told you what actually happens now | **Success.** You have primary evidence nobody else has |
| They named a person who owns the problem | **Strong success** |
| They asked what it costs | Good sign, but do not quote a price yet |
| They offered a follow-up | Success — take it, whatever the format |
| They were polite and non-committal | Normal. Most first visits are this |
| They said it would get in the way | **Valuable.** Find out exactly why |

A visit where you learn the workflow is worth more than three where you pitch. You are trying to find out whether the thing you're building should exist — not to collect signatures.

---

## The thing to remember

You are not there to impress anyone. You are there to find out what happens at 2am when a Deaf patient arrives and nobody in the building signs.

If you leave with an accurate answer to that question, the visit worked — whether or not they liked the demo.
